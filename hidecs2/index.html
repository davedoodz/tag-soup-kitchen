HIDECS 2 — Reenactment

Montreal · 2023 · product design

A 1962 program, rebuilt as an object you can touch.

HIDECS 2 was written in 1962 by Christopher Alexander and Marvin Manheim at MIT's Civil Engineering Systems Laboratory. You gave it a list of design requirements and a graph of how those requirements interacted. It cut the graph, scored each cut, and kept the grouping that severed the fewest links — the structure of the problem, computed rather than assumed.

There was no screen. Input was a card deck; output was a page of printed statements that a designer then had to redraw as a tree by hand.

The brief listed what to avoid rather than what to achieve. Alexander called those entries misfits.

The CoDEx group at McGill reenacted the system as something a visitor could operate: pick a requirement, rewire its links, watch the hierarchy recompute. My work was the physical half of that — the apparatus the software is met through.

About this screen

What you are reading is real DOM, laid out by the browser and painted onto the machine's screen. Select the text. Scroll it. The clock in the menu bar keeps time.

Transparent acrylic keeps the mechanism legible — nothing about the machine is withheld from the person using it, which is the argument a reenactment exists to make. Cork takes the glare and the noise out of the surfaces a hand actually lands on.

The keyboard was resolved in cardboard first: layer count as the variable for key travel and shoulder height, settled before cutting a material that cannot be re-cut.

Presented as a floating object rather than a desk appliance, so the hardware reads as exhibit and the software as live instrument.
